THE HONORABLE DORIS MATSUI (D-CA):  
 
Question 1: During the hearing, you described how the FCC’s news distortion policy does 
not allow the FCC to revoke or not renew a broadcast license for perceived bias. Is there 
any historical precedent or legal basis for the FCC to revoke or not renew a broadcast 
license for perceived bias?  
 
Answer:  No.  The Commission’s authority to revoke broadcast licenses is constrained by the 
First Amendment and Section 326 of the Communications Act, which specifically prohibits the 
Commission from censoring broadcasts.       
 
(a) Would the Nexstar-Tegna merger make local news more independent? Or more easily 
pressured into repeating the same message everywhere?  
 
Answer:  I believe the proposed consolidation would make the merged broadcast stations less 
independent and more easily pressured into repeating the same message everywhere.  The 
Communications Act and the Commission’s media ownership rules require broadcasters to 
operate in the public interest.  The public interest for local broadcasters has long been defined as 
facilitating localism, diversity of viewpoints and competition. 
• Localism means providing programming that serves the interests of the station’s 
community of license; 
• Diversity of viewpoints means that different broadcast station owners or ownership 
groups bring such owners’ individual priorities in what programming to deliver to their 
communities of license.  It is focused on the absence of common ownership, not any 
particular viewpoint. 
• Competition means that having a diversity of viewpoints competing to serve local 
communities will result in programming that serves the interests of the local community 
because the broadcasters need to compete for their audience. 
 
The economies of scale broadcasters intend to use to save money through consolidation include, 
among many things, the following: 
• Using single studios for broadcast for multiple stations; 
• Sharing equipment; 
• Sharing reporters across stations; 
• Sharing management personnel across stations; and 
• Stations reporting to executives that are responsible for stations across geographically 
and demographically varied communities of license.  Such management decisions are 
driven by profitably serving the corporate parent in light of all their stations. 
 
Question 2: Under Chairman Carr, the FCC reopened an investigation of CBS over how it 
chose to edit its 2024 “60 Minutes” interview with Vice President Harris, even after CBS 
complied with his request to provide the full transcript and the unedited video. Similarly, 
Chairman Carr directed the FCC to reopen investigations into ABC and NBC. All three of 
these complaints, in addition to a complaint against Fox, had been dismissed by the 
previous FCC under Chairman Jessica Rosenworcel. Chairman Carr reinstated the three 
complaints filed by a conservative group over coverage alleged to be unfavorable to the 
Trump campaign, while declining to reinstate the Fox complaint over alleged pro-Trump

election disinformation.1 It seems these investigations are selectively opened and then 
meant to stay open to provide the FCC with leverage over the content on the networks. 
News distortion requires evidence of deliberate, intentional falsification, not ordinary 
editorial judgment, correct?  
 
Answer:  Any actions that the FCC takes based on broadcaster programming must be grounded 
in the First Amendment and Section 326 of the Communications Act, which prohibits the FCC 
from censoring broadcasters. 
 
A violation of the Commission’s news distortion policy "must involve a significant event and not 
merely a minor or incidental aspect of the news report." In weighing the constitutionality of the 
policy, courts have recognized that the policy "makes a crucial distinction between deliberate 
distortion and mere inaccuracy or difference of opinion." As a result, broadcasters are only 
subject to enforcement if it can be proven that they have deliberately distorted a factual news 
report. Expressions of opinion or errors stemming from mistakes are not actionable. 
 
Question 3: Does the FCC have evidence of any deliberate, intentional falsification by CBS 
in its editing of this interview?  
 
Answer:  No.  CBS released the full video and transcript of the interview in question.  It is clear 
that CBS News made editorial decisions of the type that news organizations make every day. 
There is no evidence of deliberate, intentional falsification. 
 
(a) Do either the CBS or ABC incidents being investigated meet the standard for news 
distortion?  
 
Answer:  No.  There is no evidence of deliberately distorting a factual news report in either case.

I have not been briefed on the status of these investigations.

Answer:  Enforcement investigations are conducted by the Commission’s Enforcement Bureau. 
My requests for updates about the complaints have been refused.

Question 7: If the FCC declines to set enforceable standards, what message does that send 
to adversaries targeting U.S. networks, and what message does it send to families who 
assume their communications are secure?  
 
Answer: It sends the wrong message at the worst possible time. To adversaries, it signals that 
after the most significant telecommunications breach in our history, the United States chose not 
to adopt enforceable protections. When foreign actors are actively probing for weaknesses, 
relying solely on voluntary measures invites further testing of our defenses. 
 
To American families, it creates uncertainty. People reasonably assume that when they pick up 
the phone, send a text, or access critical services, their communications are protected by more 
than good intentions. Without clear standards and accountability in response to this attack, there 
are no benchmarks to measure network protection and no way to assess whether appropriate 
safeguards were in place after the next breach. At a time of increasing foreign cyber aggression, 
declining to act decisively sends a message of weakness to our adversaries and uncertainty to the 
American people. 
 
Question 8: How does the FCC plan to support U.S. space and satellite priorities – 
including for LEO constellations in which the U.S. leads the world – at the ITU and the 
World Radiocommunication Conference over the next two years, particularly in light of 
increasing Chinese influence and competition to develop space?  
 
Answer:  The FCC supports U.S. space and satellite priorities through  both its regulatory 
actions and its issue leadership.  The Commission’s long, bipartisan, history of supporting 
innovative space services helps it garner support among partner nations for positions that drive 
the policy outcomes to expand U.S. economic leadership.  For example, in 2022 it granted the 
first license for commercial satellite-direct-to-standard-mobile-phone service, which it followed 
in 2024 with its adoption of a framework for supplemental coverage from space. In between, its 
support for U.S. positions and advocacy at WRC23 helped set the stage for such progress. 
 
(a) What are the benefits of U.S. leadership at the ITU and WRC as they set global 
spectrum rules and satellite orbits?  
 
Answer:  The adoption of policies that support the continued growth of our satellite industry that 
is the envy of the world benefits the long term national and economic security of the United 
States. 
 
Question 9: The FCC is currently considering an open proceeding on SIP Interconnection. 
IP interconnection is key to modernizing our telecommunications networks and realizing 
some of the innovations which will improve consumer experience, enhance public safety via 
Next Generation 911, and aid in combating robocalls and robotexts. As the FCC considers 
this proceeding, how is the FCC working to ensure a seamless transition, and how do you 
plan to support these network enhancements?  
 
6 
 
Answer: IP interconnection is central to the transition to modern networks. It supports improved 
call quality, stronger robocall mitigation, and the full promise of Next Generation 911. As we 
consider this proceeding, the priority must be ensuring that the transition is seamless, reliable, 
and grounded in public safety and consumer protection. That includes providing regulatory 
clarity, avoiding destabilizing shifts, and maintaining reliability as legacy systems are phased 
out. 
 
We must also ensure interoperability, secure routing, and transparent interconnection practices so 
that modernization strengthens, rather than weakens, our networks. If done correctly, IP 
interconnection can enhance innovation, reinforce security, and improve consumer experience 
nationwide while safeguarding public safety. 
 
Question 10: Can you please speak to the importance of incorporating policies such as 
maintaining competition in rural areas and preventing anticompetitive conduct even when 
the proceeding is pending (such as a rate freeze), and setting reasonable protections for IP-
to-IP interconnection to maximize the benefits to consumers?  
 
Answer: Maintaining competition, especially in rural areas, is critical during this transition. 
Rural communities often have fewer provider choices and thinner operating margins. Even small 
disruptions or anticompetitive conduct can have outsized consequences for consumers who may 
already have limited alternatives.  
 
As this proceeding moves forward, it is important that we guard against conduct that could 
distort competition. Modernization and competition are not competing goals. They are 
complementary. If we preserve competition, particularly in rural America, and ensure fair 
interconnection practices, the transition to IP networks can expand opportunity, strengthen 
service reliability, and deliver meaningful benefits to consumers.

THE HONORABLE NANETTE D. BARRAGÁN (D-CA):  
Question 1: Commissioner Gomez, you have emphasized the importance of unlicensed 
spectrum to American innovation. Indeed, studies show Wi-Fi now carries over 85% of all 
smartphone data and will contribute an estimated $1.9 trillion to the U.S. economy this 
year. Given this massive reliance, how critical is it for the Commission to identify a steady 
pipeline of new unlicensed spectrum to support this consumer demand and innovation?  
 
Answer:  Unlicensed spectrum is a key tool for connectivity and sound spectrum management as 
well as a critical component of the digital economy.  That is why, in January 2026, I voted to 
approve expanded access to unlicensed spectrum for new devices in the 6 GHz band.   
 
Back in 2020, the FCC made an important decision to support wireless innovation and meet 
consumer demand by allowing unlicensed use of the 6 GHz Band.  At that time, wireless devices 
such as appliances, wearables, speakers, and an array of consumer electronics, connecting to the 
Internet through Wi-Fi and other standards were growing in popularity across consumer 
households.  But the bands dedicated for unlicensed connectivity were growing crowded.  To 
allow for wireless innovation to continue to reach consumers while protecting the incumbents of 
the 6 GHz band, the FCC permitted two new devices to operate in this band – Standard Power 
devices under the control of an Automated Frequency System, and Low Power Indoor devices.   
  
Since then, the popularity and types of connected devices has only continued to grow.  So, in 
2023, the FCC authorized a new class of unlicensed devices to operate in the 6 GHz band – Very 
Low Power devices. They were authorized to operate first only in a portion of the band, and later 
in 2024, throughout the entire band.  Those decisions propelled more innovation in wearable and 
Virtual Reality devices.   
  
Yet innovation inspires innovation.  There are even more devices today that will need to rely on 
unlicensed spectrum to connect consumers.  That is why, in January 2026, the FCC authorized a 
new type of unlicensed devices to operate in the 6 GHz band – Geofenced Variable Power 
devices.    
  
This new class of devices will operate at higher power, but will work with a geofencing system 
to minimize the possibility of harmful interference and stay out of exclusion zones.  The FCC’s 
decision on further unlicensed access to the 6 GHz band, demonstrates that sound spectrum 
policy can choose both innovation and protecting licensed incumbents and sends a message 
beyond our borders that we are committed to next-generation Wi-Fi innovation.
